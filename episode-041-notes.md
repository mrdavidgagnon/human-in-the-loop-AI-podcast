# Episode 041 — Beyond the Dashboard: Whose Knowledge Counts?

Playful, dramatized interview with fictional correspondent Dr. Rowan Field on a single new study in which six Dutch primary mathematics teachers spent more than a year co-designing their own learning analytics dashboard — and asked not for a better chart, but for the system to recognise evidence it currently cannot see.

Dr. Rowan Field is a fictional composite research correspondent, not a real person, and no author was interviewed. This episode is an AI-generated summary and paraphrase of the source below, useful as orientation and not a substitute for reading it.

## What the episode covers

- **The actionability gap reframed as epistemic, not technical.** Dashboards (LADs) are widely promoted for improving instructional decision-making yet continue to fall short of teachers' needs and classroom realities. The paper's central diagnosis: teachers cannot act on data that does not correspond to the categories through which they understand learning, so redesigning the visualisation layer cannot close a gap that sits in the data ontology. Current systems privilege automatically extractable digital activity — clickstreams, log data, completion rates, error frequencies — which the authors read through value-sensitive design (Friedman et al.) as a non-neutral set of choices about what learning consists of, made by researchers and developers rather than teachers.
- **Design and participants.** Qualitative phenomenological study (following Creswell) reporting the first two phases of a broader design-based research project, run over more than a year. Six Dutch primary school mathematics teachers, purposively sampled for direct experience with digital mathematics LMSs in Dutch primary education; four of six women, all holding a bachelor's degree, four with 10+ years' experience, four teaching sixth grade and two third/fourth. Phase 1: 2.5-hour brainstorming session with background questionnaire, interviews on current dashboard challenges, teacher artefacts and researcher observations; 2-hour knowledge-transfer sessions building analytics literacy; 1-hour data-driven sessions on the tools used in their own lessons. Phase 2: 2-hour design-thinking session with storyboards and a 2-hour low-fidelity prototyping session. Interviews in Dutch, audio-recorded with consent, ~1.5 hours each. Content analysis with inductive coding; two researchers coded a subset independently and reconciled by iterative dialogue to full agreement; thematic saturation when no new codes emerged.
- **Trustworthiness safeguards, stated in the episode.** Data triangulation across interviews, observations, artefacts, think-alouds and questionnaires; researcher triangulation across collection and analysis; member checking of findings and interpretations with participants; thick description of context. The authors disclose that researchers acted as both observers and facilitators and that researcher presence may have influenced participants' reflections and design proposals, mitigated by open-ended questioning and by avoiding promotion of predetermined dashboard functionality.
- **Phase 1 — what was broken.** Four of six reported difficulty tracking advancement through learning objectives and identifying gaps in mathematical understanding beyond grades. Three named decentralisation of student data across multiple LMSs as structural; the complaint was not data inaccuracy but the absence of a per-student overview across tools, including a case where two main systems reported different test results and different progress for the same student with no guidance on reconciling them. Two raised difficulty personalising instruction across gifted, average and struggling students.
- **The "super-tools" prompt (after Holstein, McLaren, and Aleven).** All six converged on a dashboard that identifies and alerts them to key student learning moments — one wanting to detect the exact moment confusion started, another wanting a per-student signal when a student is functioning differently than usual or expected. Four of six additionally asked for guidance on what the data implies for action (interpretation and next step: remedy or deepening). Two raised students' emotional states as a core rather than supplementary monitoring dimension, including a request for student emotional check-ins and an argument that emotional well-being is a precondition for learning. Authors' reading: teachers conceptualise meaningful learning evidence as inherently multi-dimensional and do not separate cognitive performance from emotional readiness.
- **Phase 2 — teacher sketches and the analogue claim.** All six sketches position the teacher as an interpretive agent rather than a passive recipient, including the most automation-oriented ("arithmetic buddy") design. Three of six incorporated paper-based and analogue inputs: uploading the teacher's own notes about students, and scanning students' paper exercises. The authors escalate this from a feature request to an epistemic claim — that understanding a student through handwritten work, and professional observational knowledge of socio-emotional states, have a standing the digital system does not currently recognise — and reframe holistic data integration as a question of who has authority to determine what counts as educationally meaningful evidence. The episode explicitly challenges the distance travelled from six teachers' sketches to "epistemic justice in educational AI" and Rowan concedes it is interpretation rather than measurement.
- **The alerting / advising / intervening typology.** Built on van Leeuwen, Rummel, and van Gog's dashboard-role taxonomy and Molenaar's six levels of automation, extended from what dashboards can do to how teachers position themselves relative to system authority. Alerting: the dashboard as perceptual extension, all interpretive and decisional authority retained by the teacher. Advising (most prevalent, four of six): the system flags and offers interpretive support and suggested actions, teacher retains final authority — "I would like hints on what to do, but then I want to leave the choice to the teacher themselves." Intervening (one teacher, conditional): system action subject to teacher pre-checking, cancellation or modification — read in the paper as positioning the teacher as reviewer of system-proposed action rather than initiator, and dependent on transparency about the system's reasoning. The authors propose that stances are situationally negotiated rather than fixed, with acceptable system authority expanding under time pressure and cognitive overload.
- **The three prototype requirements.** (1) Alerting on currently unreported dimensions — effort, self-regulation and accuracy named as the three pillars, with five of six adding effort and four naming resilience and commitment; the paper's sharpest design observation is that students who most need attention are least likely to signal it, so a dashboard that only visualises without alerting reproduces and potentially amplifies existing inequalities in teacher attention. (2) Custom clustering, found useful and currently missing by all, but surfacing an unresolvable pedagogical disagreement — four preferring same-level groupings for targeted intervention, two preferring mixed-level groupings for peer learning — which the authors argue is not resolvable through better data or algorithms because it reflects genuinely different pedagogical theories, positioning clustering as a tool for enacting instructional philosophies. (3) Feedback interventions, where four of six preferred the dashboard inform and the teacher formulate the feedback; automated feedback is characterised not merely as less efficient but as substituting a mediated message for a relational act, with three teachers raising performative-dependency concerns, and one teacher voicing the counter-position that well-implemented automated feedback has motivational and relational potential.
- **The co-design process as finding.** All teachers evaluated it positively, naming control over eventual use and epistemic gains from shared reflection; three named its time-consuming character as the primary concern, one situating it in a teacher shortage. The authors argue the time burden should be seen not as a limitation to minimise but as a structural feature of the method's generative capacity, with implications for institutional resourcing. The episode pushes back on this move explicitly, noting that "the burden is intrinsic to the value" is a claim no teacher in the study made.

## Evidence boundaries

- **Six purposively sampled teachers, Dutch primary mathematics, one educational level, one subject, one broader project.** The authors state this limits generalisability and transferability to other educational levels, institutional contexts and disciplinary domains, and that statistical representativeness was not sought — the aim was contextually grounded, pedagogically specific insight with high ecological validity. The denominator is stated in the episode before any finding is reported.
- **No learning outcomes were measured.** No claim in the paper or the episode reaches student achievement; the study covers teachers' reasoning, design articulations and prototype reactions only.
- **The prototype is low-fidelity, not a deployed system.** Reactions are to storyboards and paper prototypes, not to use in live classrooms.
- **TAM and NPS figures are formative indicators only.** Perceived usefulness mean 4.14, ease of use 4.19, behavioural intention 3.92, medians 4; NPS 17 (two promoters, three neutrals, one detractor). The authors state explicitly that given the exploratory nature and small participant number these instruments were not used to produce statistically generalisable findings or predictive adoption claims, and are interpreted descriptively alongside qualitative evidence. The episode states the n and the limit in the same breath as the numbers.
- **The intervening stance rests on one teacher, conditionally.** The authors themselves frame it as a possible boundary condition marking the outer limit of teacher-delegated system authority in this dataset, and call the typology exploratory and tentative, warranting further empirical investigation across larger and more diverse cases. The episode flags the n=1 basis in dialogue rather than letting the typology carry unearned weight.
- **Researcher dual role disclosed.** Researchers facilitated the design activities they studied; the authors acknowledge possible influence on participants' reflections and proposals.
- **The epistemic-justice and value-sensitive-design argument is interpretation, not measurement.** The episode marks it as an argument that stands or falls on persuasiveness rather than on a statistic, and distinguishes it from what the data support (that these teachers consistently proposed excluded evidence types, with pedagogical reasons).
- No quotations or statements are invented on any author's behalf. Teacher quotations read in the episode are the participants' words as recorded in the authors' published text, explicitly identified as such and not as statements made to this programme.
