## Sources

1. Cloude, Elizabeth B.; Kormann, Eva; Weichselbraun, Carina; Zhang, Kaicheng; Lindstedt, Antero; Kiili, Kristian; and Ninaus, Manuel. "Effects of Graph Stimuli and Feedback on Epistemic Emotions, Motivational Conflict, and Graph-Reading Literacy During Game-Based Learning." In *Games and Learning Alliance: 15th International Conference, GALA 2026, L'Aquila, Italy, November 17–20, 2026, Proceedings*, edited by A. Bucchiarone et al. Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 17165. Springer, Cham, 2027, pp. 126–141. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-43525-5_9 — Zotero: https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/E345Q9NG
     - Evidence: Supports that graph literacy improved moderately pre-to-post
     (Wilcoxon signed-rank, rank-biserial .40, 95% CI [.21, .55]; mean
     normalized change 10%, SD .22) among 123 Austrian secondary students
     across 13 classes, and that this improvement did **not** differ by
     feedback condition (Kruskal-Wallis, n.s.). Supports that misleading graphs
     were associated with significantly higher curiosity, surprise, confusion
     and excitement than non-misleading graphs, with task-order × graph-type
     interactions showing those differences attenuating across the eight tasks.
     Supports that elaborative feedback was associated with significantly lower
     excitement than attribution-based feedback, and with significantly higher
     motivational conflict (Mdn 78.5) than attribution-based (65) and
     evaluation-results (70) conditions — a significant difference with a
     small effect size (eta-squared .019). Supports that epistemic emotions
     predicted post-game Intrinsic Motivation Inventory subscales in
     theoretically consistent directions (boredom negative for interest,
     effort and value; confusion negative for interest; frustration negative
     for value; surprise positive for interest and value but negative for
     perceived competence).
     - Boundaries: **Every condition received inoculation-based exposure, so
     the design cannot isolate the game as the cause of literacy gains** —
     the authors state this attribution remains interpretive pending a
     non-inoculation comparison. No control group omitted the game; practice
     effects and regression to the mean are not excluded. Emotion ratings were
     collected *after* feedback delivery, so self-reports cannot cleanly
     separate reactions to the graph from reactions to the message, and
     correctness and affect cannot be causally ordered (authors' own stated
     limitation). One session, one country, one game, convenience sample;
     grades 9–11, mean age 15.03. Sex data unavailable for ~31% of the sample.
     The 14-item literacy instrument is a **modification** of the validated
     13-item Graph Literacy Scale (3 easiest items removed, 4 harder items
     added); the authors describe the combined-instrument validity evidence as
     preliminary (subscale correlations rho = .24 pre, .27 post). The authors
     report the instrument "appears insensitive to deception-detection
     specifically": correctness on misleading graphs was unrelated to pre-test
     and normalized change, while non-misleading correctness tracked literacy
     outcomes robustly. The IMI effort/importance subscale had low reliability
     (alpha = .51) and the authors advise interpreting it with caution.
     Motivational conflict was measured with a **single item** and was
     non-significant across all IMI models and the literacy-gain model; the
     authors themselves question whether it adds unique explanatory value
     beyond discrete deactivating emotions, given its rho = .74 overlap with
     boredom. Intraclass correlations of .49–.79 indicate most emotion
     variance is between-person. Mechanistic readings of *why* conflict was
     highest under elaborative feedback, and of fatigue/waning novelty behind
     the small decline in in-game correctness, are the authors' stated
     interpretations, not measured mechanisms.
     - Disclosed by the authors: funded by the European Union (ID No.
     101105874) and the Strategic Research Council (ID No. 358250), with views
     the authors' own; generative AI tools were used to revise writing and
     develop R scripts, with the authors evaluating the AI content for
     accuracy.
