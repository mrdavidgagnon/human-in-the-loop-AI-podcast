# Episode 039 — How Do You Check What You Cannot See?

Playful, dramatized interview with fictional correspondent Dr. Rowan Field on four papers from one research program, each about measuring something hidden — a bodily sensation, a student's reasoning, a discussion's shape, a dataset behind a privacy wall — and what it takes to make the proxy trustworthy.

Dr. Rowan Field is a fictional composite research correspondent, not a real person, and no author was interviewed. This episode is an AI-generated summary and paraphrase of the sources below, useful as orientation and not a substitute for reading them.

The complete citations are in the show notes, with links to the Zotero files when you have been granted access.

## What the episode covers

- **Embodied reasoning as an improvised instrument (Source 1).** Groups of three infer a mystery block's weight with no scale, using the publicly available Weights Task Dataset (video, dialogue transcripts, body-pose capture; 10 groups of three adults recruited from the Colorado State University student body, laboratory setting). Analysis focuses on the one task where outcomes diverged: 7 of 10 groups correct, 3 incorrect. Unsuccessful groups cycled back to individual sensation — strongest patterns were block-lifting returning to block-lifting (connection weight 0.433 vs 0.314) and lifting leading to swapping the tester (0.229 vs 0.129), plus repeated sharing-to-sharing dialogue (0.111 vs 0.051). Successful groups diversified embodied strategy — closing their eyes, switching blocks between hands, testing unknowns against a known weight — and their talk oriented toward monitoring and establishing accepted findings rather than trading sensations. The authors' framing: bodily sensation is not readily shareable evidence, so the work is converting private sensation into something checkable.
- **Degree of inference as the variable that predicts LLM coding reliability (Source 2).** The clean low/high split is a few-shot result (zero-shot Disagreement was κ=0.52), and boundaries are not a universal upgrade — Disagreement regressed 0.80→0.74 and the authors caution that boundaries should be used judiciously. Student peer feedback from CueThink (middle-school mathematics platform, southwestern US), human-coded and GPT-4o-coded, agreement by Cohen's kappa. Low-inference codes succeeded (Compliment few-shot κ=0.96; Disagreement few-shot κ=0.80); high-inference codes failed (Reflection few-shot κ=0.34; Reasoning few-shot κ=0.45). Adding construct boundaries — explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria — lifted Reflection to κ=0.78 and Reasoning to κ=0.81. Episode's reading: the boundaries pre-existed inside the coders' heads; the model forced them to be externalized.
- **Annotation discussions change character over their lifetime (Source 3).** Method safeguards, since the episode questions model-assisted coding elsewhere: root annotations human-coded, GPT-5 applied only to replies, a human gold standard built first at κ>0.75, and prompts iterated to clarify each relationship type's boundaries until every construct cleared κ>0.70 with a stability check. 3,052 annotations from 55 students across four graduate-level online courses at a large US research university, all on learning and data analytics, all one instructor, on the Hypothesis platform. Early annotations engage the reading directly — relating text to prior knowledge, challenging claims, proposing alternative interpretations. Later annotations engage peers — revisiting earlier comments, reconciling interpretations, addressing unresolved questions. Instructional recommendations the authors actually make: seed the early annotation space with prompts, use mid/late-stage prompts inviting critique and reconciliation, and redirect attention back to the text. The episode's grading-fairness remark is explicitly marked in-script as the show's own extrapolation, not an authors' claim — the study has no grading or assessment data.
- **Privacy protection breaking the analyst's feedback loop (Source 4).** Survey of former MORF (MOOC Replication Framework) users: 17 contacted, 13 responded (76.4% response rate), just over half (53.8%) external to the three developing institutions. Positive: half produced publishable outputs, most reported no viable alternative dataset — research that would otherwise have been infeasible. Negative: steep learning curves, documentation users called unclear, difficulty with data discovery and extraction, and constrained/delayed error reporting that made debugging dependent on the development team, because detailed errors can leak information about protected data. Also a builder-misjudgment finding: developers assumed Docker was the main barrier; users largely disagreed, some calling it a strength and two never engaging with it. Caveat raised by the authors and voiced in-script: Docker-averse researchers may never have enrolled, so they are absent from the survey. Documentation and data-quality problems partly originated with the MOOC platforms supplying data, not the MORF team. MORF's developers co-authored the survey and facilitated recruitment — disclosed in-script as partly a self-study.
- **The cross-cutting claim.** All four studies describe an inference improving when its boundary is made explicit and checkable by someone other than the person who made it; MORF is the cautionary inverse, where a boundary drawn firmly and for good reasons left researchers unable to see enough to debug.
- **A method tension named, not manufactured.** Source 3 used ChatGPT-5 for codebook theme identification and GPT-5-assisted coding of discourse moves, several of which appear high-inference by Source 2's own criterion. The episode flags this as a reader's question about method — were boundaries explicit, which codes were high-inference — and states explicitly that it is not a contradiction or a disagreement about evidence.

## Evidence boundaries

- **Three of the four sources are camera-ready conference manuscripts with no DOI assigned**, hosted as author PDFs for ICQE 2026. They are accepted-but-not-yet-formally-published; this is disclosed in the episode's opening and in each spoken citation. Only Source 4 carries a DOI and formal ACM publication.
- **Source 1:** 10 groups, one laboratory task, one university's student body. Observational pattern comparison of 7 correct versus 3 incorrect groups — no intervention, no causal claim. Authors state findings remain specific to the task and context, that task correctness does not fully capture conceptual understanding, and that successful groups may have brought relevant physics knowledge — so the strategies are not shown to be teachable.
- **Source 2:** four constructs, just over 200 lines of student data, a single LLM (GPT-4o), one platform, one subject area, one age group. Human coding is treated as the evaluation standard, so what is measured is agreement with these coders, not absolute correctness. Authors call for replication on larger datasets, broader codebooks, additional models.
- **Source 3:** differences across stages were significant for four of seven discourse codes after correction for multiple comparisons, not all seven. One instructional context, one instructor, one topic area, graduate students; authors state generalizability to other disciplines, educational levels, and annotation environments is limited. Analysis covers observable annotation behaviour only and does not reach learning or understanding. Differences are in distribution of discourse moves across stages, not learning gains.
- **Source 4:** 13 expert informants from a pool of 17 — not a representative sample. The authors' own speculation that AI coding assistants may lower some of these barriers today is clearly marked in the episode as speculation, not a finding.
- No quotations or statements are invented on any author's behalf; all attributions are paraphrase of published text with stated evidence limits preserved.
