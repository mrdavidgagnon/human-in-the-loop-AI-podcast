## Sources

1. Joseph A. Durlak, Roger P. Weissberg, Allison B. Dymnicki, Rebecca D. Taylor, and Kriston B. Schellinger. “The Impact of Enhancing Students’ Social and Emotional Learning: A Meta-Analysis of School-Based Universal Interventions.” *Child Development* 82(1), 405–432 (2011). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/2TA7HLXC) · [DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x)
   - Evidence: a random-effects meta-analysis of 213 universal school-based social and emotional learning programs involving 270,034 kindergarten through high school students. Compared with controls, the review reports better average post-intervention social and emotional skills, attitudes, positive social behavior, and academic performance, along with fewer conduct problems and lower emotional distress. Academic performance had a pooled standardized mean effect of 0.27 across 35 interventions; the authors translated that pooled effect with Cohen’s U3 index into an eleven-percentile-point distributional difference. Classroom programs delivered by teachers produced significant average effects in all six categories. Programs using sequenced, active, focused, and explicit skill-training practices and programs without reported implementation problems tended to have stronger results across several outcomes.
   - Boundary: this review covers reports through 2007, with 87% of interventions conducted in the United States. Only 47% of included studies used random assignment, so a control comparison was not always a randomized trial. Only 51% of the 726 coded outcome measures met the review’s validity criterion, meaning the study authors cited construct, concurrent, or predictive validity evidence. The review does not establish that every program works in every setting or identify settled mechanisms. Results varied substantially among studies; only 16% reported academic achievement at post-intervention, only 15% supplied qualifying follow-up data, only 32% measured social and emotional skills as an outcome, and 43% did not monitor implementation. The eleven-percentile-point figure is a distributional interpretation of the pooled standardized academic effect, not an observed eleven-point pre/post gain or a guaranteed result for an individual student, school, or program.

## Episode synthesis

The formulation “count the outcome, read the place, build the capacity” is this episode’s synthesis, not a conclusion jointly tested by the three sources. The social and emotional learning meta-analysis supplies broad outcome and implementation evidence but leaves important questions about mechanisms, variation, context, and durability. The rural cultural wealth paper offers concepts for recognizing place-based knowledge, assets, internal difference, and power but calls for empirical development. The Spencer Foundation task-force report proposes the researcher learning and institutional conditions needed for collaborative, systems-oriented inquiry but does not test their effects.

Applied to learning games or a national infrastructure for studying thinking, learning, and feeling, the synthesis suggests pairing outcome and implementation measures with contextual knowledge, community participation, ethical data governance, contestable inference, long-term relationships, and incentives for answerability. Those applications are editorial implications, not findings reported by the three sources.
