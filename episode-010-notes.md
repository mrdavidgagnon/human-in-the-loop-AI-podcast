## Sources

1. Lilach Sagiv and Shalom H. Schwartz. “Personal Values Across Cultures.” *Annual Review of Psychology*, 73, 517–546 (2022). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/WC998L9U) · [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-020821-125100)
   - Evidence: a broad scholarly review of definitions, theories, instruments, development, stability, behavioral associations, and cross-cultural evidence concerning personal values. It distinguishes evidence that values have similar meanings across cultural groups from the stronger score-equivalence evidence required to compare group means. It also compares abstract and concrete prompts, ratings and rankings, response-style controls, self-reports, implicit measures, and text-based classification.
   - Boundary: this is a narrative review integrating many research programs, not a new experiment or a single cross-cultural validation study. Evidence for similar motivational meanings does not make every instrument, translation, or group mean directly comparable. Associations between values and behavior do not make a value score a context-free prediction of an individual action.

2. Anna K. Döring, Andrea Blauensteiner, Katrin Aryus, Lisa Drögekamp, and Wolfgang Bilsky. “Assessing Values at an Early Age: The Picture-Based Value Survey for Children (PBVS–C).” *Journal of Personality Assessment*, 92(5), 439–448 (2010). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/FJSQMRS7) · [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1080/00223891.2010.497423)
   - Evidence: development and initial validation of a twenty-item picture-based values survey through child focus-group interpretation, expert content checks, and two German samples of children aged eight to twelve. A picture-only version tested with 267 children broadly recovered the expected higher-order value regions but misplaced three conservation items. A revised picture-plus-caption version tested with 421 children produced ten differentiated regions in the expected motivational circle, with power and achievement reversed.
   - Boundary: both main samples came from North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany, and most participants lived in middle-class families; neither sample was nationally representative. The study did not establish cross-cultural equivalence, representative norms, retest reliability, or convergence with behavior, parent or teacher reports, or another values instrument. The developmental interpretations of the reversed power and achievement regions were hypotheses requiring further evidence.

## Episode synthesis

The recommendation to treat a task as a conversation between construct and respondent is this episode’s synthesis. Neither source validates learning-game traces, essays, chats, or AI classifications as direct measures of personal values. Any such use would require its own evidence about response processes, score meaning, reliability, fairness, contestability, and the decisions made from the result.
