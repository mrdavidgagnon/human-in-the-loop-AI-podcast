## What the episode covers

- **The premise.** A math tutor is made of words — problem statements, hints, feedback, scaffolding prompts. So performance in a math system is partly a reading performance. Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) models mathematical mastery as a hidden state inferred from first-attempt correctness; reading ability appears nowhere in the model. The authors ask whether an unmodeled, unevenly distributed reading skill has quietly introduced unfairness nobody designed in.
- **The model under audit.** BKT as a hidden Markov process with four parameters — prior mastery, learn rate, guess, slip — implemented with pyBKT (Badrinath et al., 2021), trained with 5-fold cross-validation, slip constrained to {0, 0.05, 0.1} and guess to {0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. Correctness is defined as correct on the first attempt with no hint requested.
- **How reading ability was obtained — stated as inference, repeatedly.** There is no reading assessment in this study. An existing validated model (Almoubayyed et al., 2023a) estimates reading comprehension from a student's first interactions in MATHia's introductory workspace using four engineered feature families: first-attempt correctness, attempts per step, feedback prompts per step, and hints per step. It was trained to predict English Language Arts standardized test outcomes; the lowest 25% of predicted probabilities per grade level are labeled "emerging readers." The Gunning fog index of the introductory workspace is 7.5 (≈7th-grade reading level), which is the authors' evidence that the text does make real reading demands. The episode states the authors' own caveat aloud: the estimate "could conflate English language learner status with struggling reader status," and they "caution against using the estimations past relatively low-stakes adaptive supports."
- **Study design.** Two MATHia datasets from Carnegie Learning covering school years 2021–2022 (4,733 students) and 2022–2023 (3,816 students), 8,549 students in total, drawn from several schools in one district in the Northeastern United States, same grade level each year. Top 50 skills by number of students completing each knowledge component. Splits land slightly off 75/25 because of ties: 2021–22, 1,116 emerging (23.6%) vs 3,617 non-emerging; 2022–23, 921 (24.2%) vs 2,895 (75.8%).
- **The four fairness metrics, and why they must disagree.** Statistical parity (difference in mastery rates; 0 = fair), disparate impact (ratio of selection rates; 1 = fair), average odds (mean difference in false-positive and true-positive rates; 0 = fair), and model calibration (0 = fair). Significance by bootstrap, 10,000 iterations, 95% CIs — chosen because these metrics have no closed-form p-value. Because base rates of mastery genuinely differ between the groups, the authors state that it is impossible for the model to be fair in every way. The episode presents the result as a designer's trade-off — equal predicted mastery rates versus equally correct predictions — not as a verdict of "unfair."
- **RQ1, aggregate performance by reading group: three of four tests significant.** Observed base rates of mastery 2021–22: .687 emerging vs .776 non-emerging, difference ≈.089, 95% CI [.0747, .0950]; 2022–23: .673 vs .748, difference ≈.075. BKT *predicted* mastery 2021–22: .708 vs .752 (gap .044); 2022–23: .679 vs .733 (gap .054 — note this is one of the internally inconsistent cells; see evidence boundaries). Calibration significant but very small (.0046, CI [.0009, .0086]; .0054, CI [.0012, .0097]), slightly favouring non-emerging readers.
- **The instructive non-result: RMSE.** Not significant either year (−.0031, CI [−.0079, .0018]; −.0006, CI [−.0067, .0062]) — both intervals contain zero. The authors offer two readings and say the statistical-parity result makes the second likely: the errors are similar in magnitude but opposite in direction, which a squared, sign-blind error metric cannot distinguish from mere noise. The episode makes this the metric-selection lesson of the episode: the model's predicted gap is *smaller* than the real gap, which is what splitting the difference looks like.
- **RQ2, the headline — aggregate vs per-skill.** Aggregate 2021–22: AUC .696, RMSE .386, statistical parity −0.077, disparate impact 0.885, average odds −0.052. Aggregate 2022–23: AUC .694, RMSE .393, parity −0.066, disparate impact 0.878, average odds −0.047. The authors describe this as little unfairness in aggregate while noting it remains statistically significant given the sample size. Per skill the character changes: "geometric transform" AUC .512, parity −0.009, DI 0.991 (near-perfect parity, but coin-flip prediction — no signal to be unfair with); "identifying units" AUC .809, parity −0.175, DI 0.736; "apply exponent" AUC .771, parity −0.262, DI 0.609. **36 of the 50 skills analyzed were biased against emerging readers on both statistical parity and disparate impact; 34 of 50 on average odds** — the same direction, not scattered noise. These three rows are the worst, best and median model *in terms of statistical parity*, i.e. extremes selected on the fairness outcome itself, so no relationship between accuracy and unfairness can be read across them; the episode states that boundary explicitly and declines the inference. The authors' own explanation for the spread is reading demand: the more graphically represented "geometric transform" skill has less unfairness because skills that require more reading comprehension likely exhibit more unfairness against emerging readers.
- **RQ3, intersectional analysis, and its hedges.** District-defined demographic categories. The authors explicitly flag analyzing gender as male/female while stating that gender is not binary (citing Lindqvist et al., 2021), and combining race with ethnicity to match the district's use of "Hispanic" (citing Benthall and Haynes, 2019). Analysis restricted to White, African American and Hispanic students because other groups were too small — some under 10 students. 2021–22: 8 of 12 bootstrapped statistics significant among non-emerging readers split by race/ethnicity, but only 1 of 12 among emerging readers. 2022–23: 8 of 10 among non-emerging intersections, and unlike the prior year emerging readers *did* differ by race/ethnicity (base rates W−AA .0467 CI [.0138, .0771]; W−H .0733 CI [.0367, .1080]; H−AA −.0267 CI [−.0503, −.0042]). Flipping the split, emerging-reader status explained significant base-rate gaps *within every* race/ethnicity group in 2022–23 (W .0624, AA .0729, H .0926) and predicted-mastery gaps (W .0252, AA .0337, H .0465). The abstract's framing is that mastery rates vary by up to 10% across demographic groups once emerging-reader status is considered.
- **Why aggregation hides it.** Not by dilution of a mean — the paper runs *two distinct but related processes*: one fits a separate model per skill, the other fits models over MATHia's most common *workspaces* (groupings of skills). The aggregate row in Table 2 is that second, separately trained model, so there is no weighted-mean arithmetic to point at and no dilution to compute. The narrower and still damning statement: a model fit over pooled workspace data does not surface the spread that exists underneath it. The aggregate figure is a true answer to a question no student experiences — a student meets one skill at a time, and if it is "apply exponent," 0.609 is the relevant number.

## Evidence boundaries

- **Reading ability is inferred, not measured.** It comes from click-stream behaviour inside a math product, via a model trained to predict ELA test outcomes — not from any reading assessment administered in this study. The authors themselves warn the label may conflate English language learner status with struggling reader status and caution against use past low-stakes adaptive supports. The episode states this before any number depends on it and repeats it in the limitations pass.
- **"Emerging reader" is a bottom-25% cut per grade level** — relative by construction, not a diagnosis, and incapable of supporting any prevalence claim, since the group size was fixed before the data arrived.
- **Observational and correlational.** Log data from one district in the Northeastern United States, the same grade level, two consecutive school years, one tutoring system, one model family. No intervention, no random assignment, no learning-outcome trial. **Nothing in this design establishes that modeling reading skill would make the system fairer or improve any student's outcome** — that is the authors' implication and a reasonable one, not their finding. The episode says so explicitly.
- **The outcome variable has a reading component baked in.** Correctness means correct on first attempt with no hint, so a student who reads slowly, takes a hint, and then answers correctly is recorded identically to one who did not know the skill.
- **The fairness metrics cannot all be satisfied at once.** Because base rates genuinely differ, the authors state it is impossible for the model to be fair in every way; the paper reports a trade-off for designers rather than a verdict.
- **Significance is partly a sample-size story.** Every bootstrapped statistic is computed within a single year (n = 4,733 and n = 3,816), with model error bootstrapped at problem-solving steps rather than per student; at those sizes the bootstrap still certifies very small differences — the calibration effects (≈.005) are the clearest case. The episode states the effect size next to every significance claim. The authors' own caveat applies across the whole battery: the analyses "contain numerous comparisons that are somewhat exploratory in nature, increasing the possibility of type I error," and "future confirmatory research in more contexts is needed."
- **Per-skill coverage is incomplete.** The authors could not run all 50 skill-level models in both datasets for computational reasons; their table reports worst, median and best examples. "36 of 50" is a real count from their analysis, but the three skills named in the episode are illustrative selections, not a random sample.
- **Unreported demographic groups are not findings of no difference.** Groups too small for analysis (some under 10 students) were excluded, and one analyzed cell was too thin to use: White emerging readers in 2022–23 numbered 83, which the authors judged insufficient for a calibration comparison.
- **At least two cells in the published tables are internally inconsistent** — the 2022–23 predicted-mastery difference (.054, reported CI [.0317, .0452]) and a 2021–22 intersectional calibration cell (.0045, reported CI [.0688, .0951]) each have a point estimate outside their own confidence interval; both appear to be transcription errors in the manuscript. The episode flags both aloud, builds no claim on either, and does not silently repair them.
- **Single-cohort fairness audits are fragile.** The intersectional result reversed between the two school years: race/ethnicity barely moved the numbers among emerging readers in 2021–22 and did so clearly in 2022–23.
- No quotations or statements are invented on any author's behalf. Every number and interpretation read in the episode is paraphrase and synthesis of the authors' published text, identified as such and not as a statement made to this programme. The parallel drawn to episode 042 (shared co-authors Ocumpaugh and Nasiar, and the recurring "your instrument's resolution is a theoretical commitment" move) is identified in the episode as the correspondent's observation, not a claim either researcher has made.
