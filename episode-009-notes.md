## Sources

1. Niels G. Mede, Viktoria Cologna, Sebastian Berger, John C. Besley, Cameron Brick, Marina Joubert, Edward Maibach, Sabina Mihelj, Naomi Oreskes, and Mike S. Schäfer. “Public Communication About Science Across 68 Countries: Global Evidence on How People Get Information and Communicate About Science-Related Matters.” *OSF Preprints* (2024). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/XUBRA3M4) · [DOI and preprint](https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/xb3ha)
   - Evidence: a preregistered online survey of 71,922 respondents in 68 countries, conducted from November 2022 through August 2023. Weighted estimates were nationally representative for age, gender, and education. Respondents in 53 of 68 countries reported encountering science-related information more frequently on social media than through the other measured channels; traditional news media ranked ahead of social media in every Scandinavian and German-speaking country in the study, as well as Belgium and the Netherlands. The study also reports country-level associations between media, economic, political, and cultural indicators and self-reported information exposure or communication.
   - Boundary: this is a preprint under peer review, not a completed peer-reviewed journal article. The measures are self-reports from online samples, use broad channel categories, and have limited urban–rural representativeness. Cross-sectional country-level associations do not establish that a media system, gross domestic product, cultural classification, or freedom index caused an individual’s information behavior. The paper’s recommendation that communicators counter misinformation is a policy implication, not an intervention tested by this survey.

2. Noah Weeth Feinstein and David Isaac Waddington. “Individual Truth Judgments or Purposeful, Collective Sensemaking? Rethinking Science Education’s Response to the Post-Truth Era.” *Educational Psychologist*, 55(3), 155–166 (2020). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/QBJXK4KQ) · [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2020.1780130)
   - Evidence: a conceptual and argumentative article proposing that science education should move beyond training isolated individuals to judge scientific truth and should help people make appropriate use of science with others in social and cultural contexts. The argument distinguishes scientific knowledge from foundational certainty, emphasizes that civic decisions integrate scientific and non-scientific considerations, and treats identity, institutions, and group relations as constitutive parts of sensemaking.
   - Boundary: this article does not report a new intervention, sample, effect size, or comparative trial. Its pedagogical examples and recommendations are a theoretical agenda grounded in prior scholarship. It does not claim that collective sensemaking is automatically accurate or benign; groups can strengthen or distort knowledge, and the paper calls for more research on pedagogies that integrate civic discourse with scientific evidence.

## Episode synthesis

The proposal that public media should design for accountable collective sensemaking is this episode’s synthesis. Mede and colleagues map self-reported science-information channels and communication across countries; Feinstein and Waddington provide the conceptual argument about social sensemaking. Neither source tested a public-media platform, a learning game, an AI moderator, or a deliberative production workflow.
