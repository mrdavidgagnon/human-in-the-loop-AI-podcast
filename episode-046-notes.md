## What the paper reports

- **The claimed gap.** Existing systems place generative AI in a single role — a chatbot tutor alongside the game, a peer agent discussing the current task, a behind-the-scenes story orchestrator, or a narrative engine turning the whole game into open-ended prose. The authors argue generative AI and cognitive scaffolding have been treated as separate concerns, leaving open how AI might be woven through multiple stages of a scaffolded experience.
- **Research questions.** RQ1: how can generative AI be integrated into a physics learning game to support misconceptions and diverse learner preferences while meeting teachers' pedagogical expectations? RQ2: how do students and teachers perceive the usability, engagement potential, and educational value of the resulting game?
- **The system.** Browser-based, for students aged 12–15, laid out as a town with five locations mapped to topics: Clock Street (speed, distance and time), Bumper Cars (Newton's third law), Noisy Bridge (friction and net force), Power House (electricity and Ohm's law), Magnet Lot (magnetic forces).
- **The Bloom loop.** Seven phases per chapter: City Map (Remember) → Story (Understand) → Experiment (Apply, Analyse) → Quiz (Evaluate) → Reflection (Create) → Chat with Jules (Create) → Mission Complete (progress feedback). A Bloom-level badge increments in the interface, which the authors describe as surfacing the cognitive ladder rather than hiding it behind the narrative.
- **Jules.** A hand-drawn diegetic mascot whose pupils track the cursor, who narrates story pages, serves chapter fun facts beside the experiment, and is the visible interlocutor in the post-reflection chat. Prompt-level constraints: system message rebuilt per chapter carrying topic, concept list, collected clues and the reflection prompt, with instructions to keep replies to 2–4 sentences, use age-appropriate language, mirror the learner's language, and never reveal quiz answers. Structural constraints: chat reachable only after reflection; history kept per chapter so context cannot cross topics; replies capped at 500 tokens; quizzes and simulations hand-coded so the model never authors assessment items or physics behaviour; failed calls surface as a retryable error and are excluded from history.
- **Narrative styling.** Learner picks Original (hand-authored, static) or one of five LLM-rewritten styles — Sci-Fi, Adventure, Fairy Tale, Comedy, Mystery. One-time in-browser generation pass rewrites all five chapters' story pages through a style-specific prefix while keeping required clues and physics concepts fixed. The paper's own example: Clock Street's Original opening "The delivery timer is broken!" becomes, in Sci-Fi, an astronaut-guide framing with timing intervals between space-station beacons standing in for distance markers. Generated once per participant at session start and then held fixed — neither pre-generated nor regenerated turn by turn.
- **Validation of generated text.** Temperature 0.85 for story generation; the three chapter clues, concept list and three-page structure are pinned in the prompt and checked programmatically after generation. Output that is not a three-element array, or that omits a required clue, is rejected and regenerated; a missing clue is reinserted verbatim; after two failed retries a chapter falls back to its Original pages. Chat runs at temperature 0.7, max 500 tokens, 30-second timeout, per-chapter history. Model output is HTML-escaped before rendering.
- **Model.** A single OpenAI-compatible DeepSeek endpoint called from the browser; all narratives reported were generated in March 2026. The model identifier is a rolling alias rather than a pinned snapshot, so exact weights cannot be recovered retrospectively — the paper's stated reproducibility limitation.
- **Method.** Three stages, ethics approval from the host institution, parental consent for participants under 16. Stage 1 pre-design (n=7: five students, two teachers), scenario-based questionnaire plus semi-structured follow-ups. Stage 2 low-fidelity formative (n=4: three students, one teacher), think-aloud on a direct-instruction prototype covering three topics; participants consistently described it as "textbook-like" and "boring", which motivated the narrative redesign. Stage 3 mixed-methods summative (n=7: five students aged 12–15 of mixed prior attainment, two teachers — one traditionally- and one digitally-oriented), individual remote sessions of approximately 30–40 minutes with researcher observation by screen sharing, then the 10-item SUS; students also a 13-item post-play survey, teachers a 15-item questionnaire.
- **Usability.** Mean SUS 76.4 (range 52.5–100; teachers 91.3, students 70.5), placed in the "good" band. The lowest student score was associated with the electricity chapter, which the authors read as a signal that Power House scaffolding and interaction flow need refinement.
- **Recall and interest.** Four of five students recalled at least one formula unprompted; the fifth described the core concepts qualitatively ("force pushes back"). All five self-reported increased interest in physics. The episode marks self-reported interest as evidence about an experience, not proof of learning.
- **Transfer.** A response counted as a correct real-world application only if it named a situation outside the game world, invoked that chapter's target concept, and articulated the underlying mechanism rather than only the setting. Three of five students met all three criteria (icy-road friction, cycling travel time, trolley pushing); a fourth named a valid situation (braking on a wet road) without the mechanism; the fifth produced none. Rated by the first author. The authors read this as transfer-oriented scaffolding needing strengthening, particularly for lower-attainment learners.
- **Teachers.** Both found the system useful as a supplementary or revision tool and confirmed curriculum alignment. The traditionally-oriented teacher wanted outcome-style analytics (misconception hotspots, quiz accuracy); the digitally-oriented teacher wanted process-style analytics (hint usage, time-on-task). Neither asked for the AI companion to be removed; one liked the chat partner because it "asks back rather than answers".
- **Ten design implications (Table 1).** Target known misconceptions explicitly, not just correctness; offer multiple hint formats; scope every AI utterance to the current chapter and concept; use a diegetic character to absorb the AI's social role; let learners choose a narrative style while keeping physics fixed; couple each story problem to a manipulable simulation; make the Bloom-level progression visible; provide both outcome and process analytics for teachers; default to language mirroring; keep simulations hand-coded to bound hallucination surface.
- **Why simulations stay hand-coded.** The narrative names a town problem and the simulation must be the exact mechanism resolving it; a hand-coded simulation can be aligned to a curriculum point and audited for correctness, whereas fully LLM-generated simulations would introduce variability that could undermine conceptual consistency. A fixed simulation layer also gives Jules a stable scope to live inside. The authors frame this as a deliberate design boundary rather than a technical limitation.
- **The authors' own correction (footnote).** An earlier version of the magnet simulation and its associated quiz item described the force as following an inverse-square law. This over-simplifies dipole–dipole interaction, which falls off considerably faster, and has been corrected so the simulation now demonstrates the relationship qualitatively without asserting a power law. The authors note the error originated in the hand-authored layer rather than in model output, which underlines that constraining generative content does not by itself remove the need for expert review of authored material. The episode treats this as the paper's most useful lesson.
- **Limitations the authors state.** Small single-context sample (n=7) with no control condition, so findings are exploratory rather than conclusive. Learner-selected narrative styles introduced uncontrolled variation, so narrative-style effects cannot be separated from the overall experience. Reliance on prompt engineering and constrained interaction design rather than model fine-tuning to manage hallucination risk. Neither the generated narratives nor Jules's conversational responses were subjected to systematic review for factual correctness or appropriateness; sessions were observed in real time but conversation logs were not retained, so the authors cannot report how often Jules's explanations were accurate, and claims about the agent's reliability rest on prompt and structural constraints rather than measured output quality. No content-moderation filter and no automated correctness check over Jules's output were deployed. Reproducibility is limited by the rolling model alias.
- **Future work.** A controlled between-subjects evaluation comparing Wonder Town with and without learner-selectable narrative styling, using pre-test, post-test and delayed-retention measures with a larger cohort (target n≈30); adaptive struggle-detection inspired by prior learning-analytics research; a teacher-facing analytics dashboard combining process measures with learning outcomes; and a moderation and verification layer around Jules — transcript logging, an automated check of responses against the chapter's concept list, and expert review of a sample of generated narratives — so output quality can be measured rather than assumed.
- **Claimed priority.** To the best of the authors' knowledge, Wonder Town is the first reported educational game to integrate generative AI across multiple stages of a Bloom-scaffolded learning loop. Stated as the authors' hedged claim; the episode does not adjudicate it.

## Evidence boundaries

- **No learning gains are established.** Every quantitative figure in this episode is a usability or perception measure from n=7, or a count out of five students. The authors say so themselves and the episode repeats the denominators aloud each time.
- **The transfer counts are judgements, not test scores.** Three-of-five meeting three stated criteria, rated by the first author, with no second rater and no inter-coder agreement reported.
- **Jules's accuracy is unmeasured.** This is the episode's central point. The constraint architecture is real and specific; whether it produced accurate explanations was not assessed, and the logs that would permit assessment were not retained.
- **Bloom is a design frame here, not a validated progression.** The evaluation does not assess learners' movement across the six levels.
- **No author statements, motives, or reactions are invented.** Teacher and student phrases appearing in quotation marks ("textbook-like", "boring", "force pushes back", "asks back rather than answers", "The delivery timer is broken!") are verbatim from the paper. All 22 quoted spans in the script were verified present in the extracted source text before rendering.
- **Author/editor boundary.** Fu, Constantin, Imperatore and Zhang are the authors. The CHI PLAY Companion '26 editors are not credited as authors anywhere in the episode.
