[Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/67HF8HR8) - [DOI / publisher record](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2024.705110)

2. Jinho Jang, Seunmin Eun, Hyunwoong Lee, Jeewon Choi, and Young Hoan Cho. "The Effects of Prompt Scaffolding on Learning to Write Arguments with ChatGPT." Proceedings of the 18th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2024), pp. 1502–1505, ISLS (2024). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/YVVF3ZMF)
   - Labeled in this episode as an accepted ICLS 2024 conference paper stored in David's Zotero library; the Zotero item carries no separate DOI beyond the ICLS proceedings record.
   - Evidence: Quasi-experimental study of 179 Korean high school students (98 experiment, 81 control) over five 50-minute class sessions. The experiment group received prompt scaffolding (guidelines for asking ChatGPT questions and reflecting on responses); the control group wrote arguments with ChatGPT without scaffolding. Repeated-measures ANOVA showed significant group-by-time interactions on all four outcomes (self-efficacy F(1,177)=19.39, p<.001; interest F(1,177)=16.87, p<.001; knowledge F(1,177)=4.83, p=.029; performance F(1,177)=44.08, p<.001); main effects of group were significant only for interest (p=.006), and effect sizes were modest (e.g., post-test self-efficacy 4.31 vs 4.01 on a 6-point scale). The paper's conclusion: ChatGPT can be beneficial for learning to write only when learners effectively interact with it. Boundaries: single-implementation, quasi-experimental, Likert-scale survey and essay measures; no control over the specific ChatGPT model version; findings support a design association, not a generalizable claim about all AI writing tools.

3. Natalya St. Clair, Lynn Stephens, and Daniel Damelin. "Navigating Multidimensional Data Structures: Insights from Data Experts and Implications for Pedagogy." Working document / preprint, The Concord Consortium, ICLS 2026 (2026). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/6SWMAZ6Z)
   - Labeled in this episode as a working document: the Zotero item carries no DOI, date, or publisher record; the paper describes a 3-year NSF project (Grant DRL-2201177) still in progress.
   - Evidence: Think-aloud interviews with nine data experts (data scientists and professional analysts from business and academic settings in Canada and the U.S.) asked to make sense of unfamiliar multidimensional datasets. Three main approaches identified: (1) manipulating flat tables (long/wide formats), (2) creating relational databases (fact and dimension tables), (3) using computational commands (joins, filters in Python/SQL). Most experts described more than one approach and decided between them case by case. The paper's implication: students need tools that let them restructure data and need to be taught to see data as something they can shape. Boundaries: nine experts, exploratory study, no student data; the pedagogical implications are framed as open questions, not tested interventions.

5. David F. Feldon, Umar Shehzad, and Spenser Clark. "Reinventing Measurement Models for the Learning Sciences: Reconceptualizing Psychometrics to Accommodate Context as Intrinsic to Learning." Working document / preprint, ICLS 2026 (2026). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/9NJ23SHU)
   - Labeled in this episode as a working document: the Zotero item carries no DOI; the paper is an accepted ICLS 2026 conference paper.
   - Evidence: Mixed-methods measurement study using a national longitudinal study of 336 Ph.D. students in the biological sciences across 53 universities (8 years of surveys; 548 interview transcripts from 94 participants). Standard CFA showed substantial item parameter drift (IPD) across years. A moderated nonlinear factor analysis (MNLFA) incorporating a qualitative code (positive professional relationship with advisor, derived from thematic analysis of interviews) as a moderator reduced IPD substantially on most items and met fit criteria (RMSEA at or below .08; CFI at or above .90). Boundaries: one moderating variable, one use case, one scale; the authors note that multiple moderators and their interactions have not yet been explored; it is a demonstration, not a settled framework.

6. Max K. Sherard, Jessie Zarazaga, and Anthony J. Petrosino. "Space and Time as Elements to Design With in Engineering for Sustainability." Working document / preprint, ICLS 2025 (2025). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/59F3HXT8)
   - Labeled in this episode as a working document: the Zotero item carries no DOI; the paper is an accepted ICLS 2025 conference paper.
   - Evidence: Microethnographic interaction analysis of the first day of an engineering co-design project in Kasisa, rural Tanzania, where engineers, architects, and community members collaborated to design a sustainable roof tile using rice husks. The analysis (transcribed conversations, gestures, translations) shows that the design required collaboration between people and the material — the engineers had to reveal and revise assumptions about the spatial and temporal trajectory of rice husks, and community members taught them those things. The paper argues that sustainability-focused engineering co-design requires spatial, temporal, and material reasoning, and that local knowledge is a co-designer in the loop, not a resource to be extracted. Boundaries: one village, one material, one day; case study, not a general method.
