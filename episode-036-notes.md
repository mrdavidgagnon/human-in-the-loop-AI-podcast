## Sources

1. Sofia Tancredi and Martha W. Alibali. "Symbol Grounding and Re-Grounding in Balance and Movement: Insights From Children's Mathematical Compositions." Proceedings of the International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2026), ISLS; University of Wisconsin–Madison (2026). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/P62Y49LV) - [ISLS repository record](https://repository.isls.org/handle/1/12685)
   - Labeled in this episode as an accepted conference paper stored in David's Zotero library as a working document: the Zotero item carries no DOI, date, or publisher record. The full text is the ICLS 2026 long paper (pp. 783–792) in the ISLS open repository; the 2026.isls.org site lists it as a 2026 Outstanding Paper.
   - Evidence: Design-based, microgenetic multimodal video analysis of 9 semi-structured, task-based clinical interviews (grades 1–6, 44–74 min, median 60) with the Balance Number Line (a physical balance board with a number line). Findings: children grounded numbers both to balance states (positive/negative integers symmetric around 0) and to hand-movement trajectories on the number line (operations left-to-right), integrating the two; and children dynamically re-grounded symbolic expressions at moments of mathematical transition and communicative difficulty (the documented example is a child explaining to the researcher). Grounding was selective — one child did not rock at all and children often reduced number-line referencing — and the authors frame the findings as suggestive rather than universal. Supports the claim that symbol grounding is dynamic and contextual rather than a one-way concrete-to-abstract progression. Boundaries: 9 children, single developing design; no comparison condition; it does not establish that balance-based instruction outperforms other instruction or that all children ground this way.

2. Max Sherard, Candace Walkington, LeaAnne Daughrity, Prajakt Pande, Theo Beauchamp, Anthony Petrosino, and Anthony Cuevas. "Pedagogical Issues in Virtual Reality Mathematics Education." Proceedings of the 18th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2024), pp. 426–433, ISLS (2024). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/NQ38P3AD) - [DOI / publisher record](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2024.705110)
   - Evidence: Video-recorded study of 16 middle school students across seven VR mathematics tutoring sessions using immersive, dynamic, collaborative, remotely accessed HMDs; analyzed with the Social Infrastructural Framework (Bielaczyc, 2006). Identified three pedagogical issues: (1) classroom management via surveillance and isolation, (2) the avatar system and social identity, (3) management of virtual bodies (mobility and gesture), with a remedies discussion. Boundaries: small qualitative single-city study (a private university and two public middle schools in one U.S. Southwest city; seven weeks, 45 recorded sessions); it documents what teachers must manage when the body goes virtual — it is not evidence that VR tutoring produces better math learning.

3. Zachary (Zach) Ryan. "Playful Frameworks of Research: Play and Its Potential in Researching Learning Alongside Youth." Proceedings of the 18th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2024), pp. 809–816, ISLS (2024). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/86BAG6ET) - [DOI / publisher record](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2024.498749)
   - Evidence: Conceptual/design paper framing play (via Vygotsky's sociocultural definition) as a boundary practice in participatory design research with youth, addressing the power imbalance between youth and adult stakeholders. Offers three dimensions where play fits the research process: selection of methods (sites, topics, data collection); participatory design projects aimed at material community change; and co-analysis of qualitative video data alongside youth. Ryan states that play is not inherently equitable — without criticality it can reify hegemonic power dynamics — and frames the paper as a beginning of a dialogue. Boundaries: a framework and call for the field, not a study with a sample; no outcome evidence that playful research processes produce better findings.

4. Miwa Aoki Takeuchi, Sophia Thraya, and Tatenda Mambo. "Learning to See Beyond Anthropocentric Views on Bodies Through Transdisciplinary Soil-Centered Pedagogy." Proceedings of the 18th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2024), pp. 987–990 (short paper), ISLS (2024). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/CHTXZQGE) - [DOI / publisher record](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2024.388000)
   - Evidence: Social design research documenting Soil Camp, a summer camp research–design partnership on a community urban farm (2020 origin with a refugee resettlement agency; three iterations 2021–2023) centering intergenerational land-based wisdoms of communities with forced-displacement experiences. 94 children aged 5–15, 33 facilitators (incl. 20 teachers/teacher candidates), 157 hours 19 minutes of video analyzed collectively. Findings: the notion of "body" expanded, as illustrated by selected case snapshots (a child's mushroom-body drawing, another's soil-heart drawing) showing children and teachers coming to see interconnectedness between human and nonhuman bodies. Boundaries: multi-year program documentation reporting illustrative snapshots from selected children, not an outcome trial with a control group; the widened category of "body" is the finding, not a measured learning gain.

5. Jinho Jang, Seunmin Eun, Hyunwoong Lee, Jeewon Choi, and Young Hoan Cho. "The Effects of Prompt Scaffolding on Learning to Write Arguments with ChatGPT." Proceedings of the 18th International Conference of the Learning Sciences (ICLS 2024), pp. 1502–1505 (short paper), ISLS (2024). [Open in David Gagnon's Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/field_day/items/EWSKSSEU) - [DOI / publisher record](https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2024.831011)
   - Evidence: Quasi-experimental study with 179 Korean high school students (98 experiment, 81 control) writing argumentative essays with ChatGPT across five 50-minute sessions; the experiment group received scaffolding on creating prompts (not on the essay itself), and both groups used ChatGPT. The experiment group showed larger over-time improvements in self-efficacy, interest, knowledge, and argumentative writing performance (significant group-by-time interactions; group main effects were not significant for several measures). Authors' stated implication: ChatGPT is beneficial for learning only when learners effectively interact with it (do the cognitive framing work themselves). Boundaries: single context (Korean high school, argumentative writing, one tool); no measure of harm from outsourcing (both groups used the tool); not generalizable to all subjects, ages, or AI systems.
