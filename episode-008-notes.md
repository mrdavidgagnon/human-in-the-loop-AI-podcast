## Sources

1. Sandra L. Woolley, Woan-Jue J. Benjamin, and Anita Williams Woolley. “Construct Validity of a Self-Report Measure of Teacher Beliefs Related to Constructivist and Traditional Approaches to Teaching and Learning.” *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 64(2), 319–331 (2004). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/KU5MT7GF) · [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403261189)
   - Evidence: instrument-development study using interviews, pilot exploratory factor analyses with 61 preservice and 137 in-service elementary teachers, and confirmatory analysis with 896 preservice teachers. The proposed four-factor structure was not confirmed; a post-hoc 21-item, three-factor revision fit somewhat better, with uneven internal consistency. This supports preliminary structural evidence and further revision, not claims about observed teaching, teacher effectiveness, student learning, individual evaluation, or generalization beyond one Pennsylvania institution and region.

2. Bruce Johnson and Robert McClure. “Validity and Reliability of a Shortened, Revised Version of the Constructivist Learning Environment Survey (CLES).” *Learning Environments Research*, 7(1), 65–80 (2004). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/KVI2GJ79) · [DOI and publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1023/B:LERI.0000022279.89075.9f)
   - Evidence: instrument-revision study using responses from 290 preservice and in-service science teachers, most of whom were additional instrument-analysis respondents rather than members of the project’s small longitudinal teacher cohort. The study used exploratory factor analysis, internal-consistency analysis, and limited item-comprehensibility evidence from relatively few self-selected written comments and informal remarks; it did not conduct systematic cognitive interviews. The team shortened the teacher survey from 30 to 20 items while retaining five conceptual scales. Later student samples of 110 and 354 showed good internal consistency, but the article reports no independent exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis of the revised structure, no test–retest evidence, and no revised teacher-form reliability. The article supports use as one contributor to classroom profiles, especially alongside interviews and observation, not a comprehensive or causal account of classroom practice.
   - Source-internal anomaly: visual inspection of the article PDF confirms that Table I appears to transpose the descriptions of Shared Control and Student Negotiation. Appendix B’s actual revised items use the established meanings—planning and instructional control for Shared Control, and peer explanation for Student Negotiation. The episode follows the item content rather than the apparently swapped table descriptions.

3. Sarah M. Wolff, Glynis M. Breakwell, and Daniel B. Wright. “Psychometric Evaluation of the Trust in Science and Scientists Scale.” *Royal Society Open Science*, 11(4), article 231228 (2024). [Open in the Field Day Zotero library](https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/field_day/items/R3GRBKRS) · [DOI and open-access article](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.231228)
   - Evidence: psychometric reanalysis of five samples drawn from four earlier studies: United Kingdom, 648; Portugal, 486; Germany, 198; 2014 United States undergraduates, 344; and a 2021 United States Prolific sample quota-matched on age, sex, race, and ethnicity, 1,014. The analytical total was 2,690. The methods prose reports 314 for Portugal, but Table 2 and the authors’ OSF data contain 486 complete Portuguese cases; the episode does not rely on the disputed prose count. A one-factor model fit poorly; a correlated two-factor model organized by positive versus reverse-scored wording fit better, though it remained below conventional fit criteria in the German and 2014 United States samples and interpretation of the split remained unsettled. Configural invariance was supported between the 2021 United States and United Kingdom samples, but neither metric nor strong/scalar invariance was supported; comparisons of underlying factor relationships or average levels were therefore not justified. The study does not rank national trust, prove that wording alone caused the structure, or establish one universally transportable trust score.
