1. Vandenberg, Jessica; Mott, Bradford; Penilla, Carlos; Lester, James; and Ozer, Elizabeth. "Participatory narratives for AI learning: Integrating self-efficacy, agency, and ethical reflection." *Proceedings of the 20th International Conference of the Learning Sciences — ICLS 2026*, pp. 2801-2803. International Society of the Learning Sciences, 2026. DOI: https://doi.org/10.22318/icls2026.571004 — repository record: https://repository.isls.org/handle/1/13060 — Zotero: https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/S4UUS34F
     - Source type: **peer-reviewed conference short paper**, three pages in a
     proceedings. It has a DOI and a publisher of record, so it is not an
     unpublished working document; but the format supports a theoretical
     argument plus an early signal from a study, not a full empirical report.
     Author affiliations: Vandenberg, Mott and Lester at North Carolina State
     University; Penilla and Ozer at the University of California San Francisco.
     - Evidence: Supports a **conceptual/design contribution** — a
     theory-driven design framework grounded in Social Cognitive Theory and
     informed by participatory learning perspectives, which connects SCT and
     SCCT's motivational constructs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and
     goals) with participatory mechanisms the paper names as role-based
     engagement, consequential decision-making, and ethical reflection. The
     paper's distinctive theoretical move is to treat participatory learning as
     *elaborating the mechanisms* through which the social cognitive constructs
     are experienced and developed in practice, rather than as a rival account
     of motivation. Supports a description of the implemented environment,
     AI4HEALTH, a narrative-centered learning environment in which learners
     take the role of a medical intern investigating virtual patient cases with
     AI diagnostic tools, guided by a virtual mentor, analysing AI models
     trained on X-ray data from varied datasets so that bias, fairness and
     reliability in AI-assisted diagnosis arise as properties of the learner's
     own instrument. Supports three qualitatively identified mechanisms from
     the pilot: enacting self-efficacy through role-based participation;
     negotiating AI understanding through reflective ethical reasoning
     (students discussed trade-offs between technical accuracy and social
     impact); and projecting possible selves through narrative trajectories.
     Supports the paper's explicitly stated **design conjecture** as a testable
     hypothesis.
     - Boundaries — study design: An **exploratory pilot study**, so described
     by the authors. n = 25 middle school students, ages 12-14, one 30-minute
     gameplay episode, followed by four structured focus groups of
     approximately 20 minutes each. Data sources were gameplay logs (diagnostic
     decisions and dialogue selections), researcher observations, and focus
     group discussions; analysis was inductive thematic analysis. **No pre-test
     or post-test, no control or comparison condition, and no measure
     administered before and after gameplay are reported anywhere in the
     paper.** Therefore no claim of change, gain, or effect is established by
     this design — including the self-efficacy claim, which rests on students'
     reports of increased confidence rather than on any instrument. No measure
     of conceptual AI understanding is reported, so the claim that ethical
     tension supported deeper engagement with core AI concepts is a reading of
     focus-group talk, not an assessed outcome. No follow-up beyond the single
     session is reported, so durability is not addressed.
     - Boundaries — self-report and setting: the central evidence is what
     students said about themselves, in groups, to the researchers who built
     the environment, immediately after playing it and having been invited to
     discuss it. Social desirability, demand characteristics, group dynamics
     and novelty all push in the same direction; none is estimated or
     controlled. Twenty-five students support no claim of
     generalisability, and the authors do not make one.
     - Boundary — one phrase outruns the design: the findings section states
     that the students' career projections *extended beyond gameplay*, with
     students articulating emerging interests and aspirations. The only
     post-gameplay data collection the paper reports is the focus group, which
     it says simply followed the gameplay; it reports no interval and no
     follow-up of any kind. So what the design supports is that students talked
     about future careers after they stopped playing — not that an interest
     persisted. The episode names this distinction aloud and attributes the
     reservation to Rowan's reading of the paper's own timeline. Credit where
     due: the paper's formal claims use *may* and *suggests*, and its
     concluding framing is explicitly a design conjecture; this is the one
     place in the findings where the wording invites a stronger reading than
     the design allows.
     - Boundary — equity claim: the paper suggests that early, context-rich
     exposure to AI applications, particularly in socially relevant domains
     such as health, **may** support broader and more equitable participation
     in emerging STEM pathways. The paper reports **no demographic composition
     for its 25 participants** — no breakdown by gender, race, socioeconomic
     status, or prior computing exposure. So the equity hypothesis is advanced
     on a sample whose composition is not described, and no evidence in this
     paper bears on equitable participation. It is honestly hedged as a
     suggestion; it is not a finding.
     - Boundary — theoretical contribution: the paper positions itself as
     contributing to theory-building at the intersection of motivation and
     participation, illustrating how participatory narrative design can serve
     as a bridge between psychological and sociocultural perspectives on
     learning. As a conceptual contribution the integration is coherent and
     designable-from. As an empirical contribution it is not established: a
     bridge between traditions would be validated by showing the combined
     account predicts something neither predicts alone, which requires
     measurement this study did not perform.
     - Structural facts, disclosed without inference: (a) **Self-citation of
     the interpretive lens.** The mechanism through which finding 1 is read —
     mastery experience as a key source of self-efficacy development — is cited
     to Ozer and Bandura (1990); Elizabeth Ozer is the paper's fifth author. The
     episode states this as a fact about the analysis (the theoretical lens
     applied to the qualitative data is one of the paper's own authors' prior
     work, and the paper does not report who performed the analysis) and draws
     no inference about anyone's state of mind. Self-citation of this kind is
     ordinary and is arguably the point of including a self-efficacy expert on
     the team. (b) **Funding.** Stated in the acknowledgements: the National
     Institute of General Medical Sciences of the National Institutes of Health
     under award R25GM154357, with additional support from the Health Resources
     and Services Administration of the U.S. Department of Health and Human
     Services under cooperative agreements U8DMC45901; the paper carries the
     agencies' standard disclaimers. Both are health-workforce mechanisms,
     which is the visible and ordinary explanation for a medical-intern setting

     between mechanism and design and alleges nothing.
