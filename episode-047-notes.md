# Episode 047 — Which Kind of Bad Feeling Are You Measuring?

## Sources

1. Edlinger, Moritz; Huber, Stefan E.; and Ninaus, Manuel. "Beyond Fun: Negative Emotions Predict Pro-environmental Attitudes and Behaviour in a Persuasive Ecogame." In *Games and Learning Alliance: 15th International Conference, GALA 2026, L'Aquila, Italy, November 17–20, 2026, Proceedings*, edited by A. Bucchiarone et al. Lecture Notes in Computer Science, vol. 17165. Springer Nature Switzerland AG, 2027, pp. 97–111. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-43525-5_7 — Zotero: https://www.zotero.org/groups/2412036/items/96X8SZPF
   - Author affiliations: Moritz Edlinger, Stefan E. Huber and Manuel Ninaus — Department of Psychology, University of Graz, Graz, Austria; Manuel Ninaus also — LEAD Graduate School and Research Network, University of Tübingen, Tübingen, Germany.
   - **Design.** A laboratory study analysing one of the three experimental groups of a larger project (233 adults recruited) examining post-gameplay reflection — specifically the group that received **no** post-gameplay reflection. Within that slice the emotion-to-outcome analysis is **correlational**: four OLS regressions per outcome (baseline pre-attitude model, then DAS, AFFDEX and GEQ extended models), testing whether emotional variables explain variance beyond baseline attitudes. R version 4.5.1; significance level .05.
   - **Sample.** 79 recruited in Austria via university-wide email, word of mouth, campus notices and local subreddit posts; participants were told only that they would play a commercially available video game. Eligibility: ages 18–65, native German proficiency or C1-level German fluency (all materials in German); no prior gaming experience required. Twelve excluded — 4 failed attention checks (instructed response items), 1 did not finish within the booked lab slot, 7 technical issues such as game/setup crashes — leaving **n = 67**. 39 identified as male, 28 as female; 52 were university students; mean age 25.78 (SD 6.07, range 18–49). Mean playing time 64.7 min (SD 8.54, range 51.9–87.9).
   - **What it supports.** (a) A significant pre-post increase in palm-oil-related pro-environmental attitudes, t(66) = 6.18, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.49, CI 95% [0.31, 0.60]; M_pre 5.02 (SD 0.94) to M_post 5.48 (SD 0.89) on a 7-point scale — described by the authors as a small-to-moderate increase. (b) Substantial donations: €633 total, ≈ €9.45 per person, about 45% of the €20 compensation (€360.50 to the Gibbon Conservation Society, €144.00 to WWF, €128.40 to the Jane Goodall Institute). (c) Self-reported negative-emotion change (DAS) significantly predicted post-game attitudes (estimate 0.27, SE 0.11, t = 2.48, p = .016; model R² = .66) and donation amount (estimate 3.31, SE 1.64, t = 2.01, p = .048; model R² = .12, p = .040), beyond baseline attitudes; positive-emotion change did not (attitudes p = .548; donations p = .295). (d) Neither AFFDEX facial indices nor GEQ post-hoc experience scores significantly increased explained variance in either outcome.
   - **What it does not support.** It is **not** a controlled causal test of the game's effect: this analysis uses the no-reflection group with a pre-post comparison and no control condition, so the attitude change cannot be attributed cleanly to gameplay. The emotion findings are **correlational within an experiment** — the paper does not manipulate emotion. Effect sizes are modest and the donation result sits just under threshold (p = .048), with the DAS model explaining about 12% of donation variance; the self-reported emotion scores added ≈ 4.2% explained variance for attitudes and ≈ 9.2% for donations over baseline. **Depth of processing was not measured**: the Elaboration Likelihood Model is the authors' explanatory frame, not a finding. The **null** results for AFFDEX and GEQ are evidence about those operationalisations — session-averaged facial indices and a post-hoc questionnaire administered after several other instruments — and the authors explicitly state the facial-analysis failure should not be read as evidence that automated emotion detection is unimportant. Generalisability is limited by a single commercial game and a predominantly student Austrian/German-language sample; power is limited for finer-grained analyses (specific facial action units, mediational models, discrete emotions); donations are one form of pro-environmental behaviour and neglect individual differences in preferred engagement such as lifestyle changes or activism; and non-linear effects (too little or too much emotion leading to disengagement) remain untested.
   - **Influential-case handling.** One participant showed a large standardised residual (|z| > 3), high leverage and elevated Cook's distance, and was one of the five who reported disliking the game. The authors retained the case as theoretically informative; a sensitivity analysis excluding it yielded a **larger** attitude effect (Cohen's d rising from 0.49 to 0.62) with direction and significance of key effects unchanged. The reported headline is therefore the more conservative figure.
   - **Donation-procedure safeguard.** Donation instructions were presented in a closed portfolio; participants decided in a separate area partitioned by a room divider and returned the closed portfolio to the experimenter before leaving the room, to minimise experimenter influence. This mitigates but does not eliminate social desirability, which the authors retain as a limitation.
   - **Declared interests and AI use.** The acknowledgments state that the authors thank Broken Rules — the studio that developed the game under test — for a €1,000 donation supporting independent research and participant donations to environmental NGOs, and that the donation did not influence the pre-determined game choice or study design and that the study was not commissioned. Game selection was based on the authors' prior player-review analysis (source 2 below). The authors separately declare no competing interests relevant to the content of the article. The acknowledgments also state that generative AI (GPT-5.1) was used for language editing and that the authors remain responsible for the content. The episode reports these as disclosed structural facts and attributes no motive to any author.
   - **Game under test.** *Gibbon: Beyond the Trees*, developed by Broken Rules, released 2022 — described in the paper as a 2D hand-drawn ecological adventure in side-scrolling-platformer form with an approximately one to one-and-a-half hour narrative, in which the player is a gibbon in the Malaysian jungle and the setting transforms from dense jungle to barren wastelands and overcrowded residential neighbourhoods. Persuasive mechanics identified by the authors: **Meaningful Feedback** (swinging becomes harder and more frustrating as the jungle is replaced by deforested, industrialised terrain) and **Perspective-Taking** (a non-human animal vantage point). This characterisation is the authors' design analysis, not a measured variable. 62 of 67 participants reported liking the game and would recommend it — a like–dislike ratio of about 92.5%, which the paper notes is comparable to the game's 91% positive rating on Steam (May 2026).
