**Host:** The complete citation first, before the cleverness.

**Rowan:** Karen Schrier, editor, *Learning, Education, and Games, Volume 3: 100 Games to Use in the Classroom and Beyond*, published by ETC Press at Carnegie Mellon University in 2019. This is the only source for this episode.

**Host:** What can an edited collection of one hundred games do that a controlled comparison cannot?

**Rowan:** It can widen design imagination. The volume presents varied games and classroom possibilities, helping teachers ask what a game affords: perspective-taking, rehearsal, collaboration, systems reasoning, ethical discussion, or practice with a representation.

**Host:** So it is a map of possibilities, not a giant experiment with a hundred treatment groups.

**Rowan:** Exactly. The book is a practitioner-facing collection of examples and design cases. It does not give all one hundred games a shared causal effect, common sample, or common outcome measure.

**Host:** What should a teacher ask before downloading a game?

**Rowan:** First, what precise learning goal warrants the activity? Second, what does successful play require, and is that genuinely related to the goal? Third, what support is inside the game, and what support must a teacher or peer provide?

**Host:** The game is not the lesson plan wearing pixels.

**Rowan:** Right. A game becomes educational through its relationship with goals, learners, facilitation, time, and follow-up. Debriefing, explanation, revision, and transfer may be where experience becomes disciplinary understanding.

**Host:** And accessibility?

**Rowan:** Ask who cannot participate as the design assumes. Access includes hardware, language, motor and sensory demands, familiarity with game conventions, emotional safety, and time. Difficulty may reveal a barrier in the interface or activity, not a deficit in the learner.

**Host:** Suppose students love the game and the room is lively. Is that enough?

**Rowan:** It is enough evidence to investigate further, not enough to finish the argument. Ask learners to explain a mechanism without the game’s cues, try a new case, compare explanations, and notice whose voice shaped the group answer.

**Host:** So the evidence plan comes before the triumphant screenshot.

**Rowan:** Ideally. Decide what observation would support the learning claim and what would weaken it. Do not let whatever the platform happens to log quietly define the construct.

**Host:** What does the book not establish?

**Rowan:** It does not establish that game-based learning is one treatment, that every listed game works, or that examples generalize across classrooms. Each claim stays attached to its chapter, setting, learners, and facilitation.

**Host:** Give us the refrigerator magnet.

**Rowan:** Let the book widen the design imagination. Let the teacher align the activity. Let learners explain what happened. Let the evidence remain local and contestable.
