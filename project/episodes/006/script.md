**Host:** What is the central challenge?

**Rowan:** Designing a game that is playful while also eliciting evidence useful for formative assessment. The project’s game, Beats Empire, places middle-school learners in the role of music producers who use data representations to make decisions about artists, songs, and markets.

**Host:** Formative means the evidence changes what happens next, not merely what gets recorded.

**Rowan:** Exactly. The book follows interdisciplinary design, implementation, analysis, and reflection. Gameplay is treated as a setting for eliciting evidence about computational thinking with data, alongside information intended to support teacher decisions.

**Host:** Why not just say that successful play equals mastery?

**Rowan:** Because a game action can have several explanations. A learner may understand the data, guess, exploit a mechanic, misunderstand the interface, or collaborate with a peer. Rich telemetry can make a larger haystack without producing a better needle.

**Host:** What makes an assessment game different from a game that happens to collect clicks?

**Rowan:** The design starts with the claim. What should learners know or be able to do? What task can genuinely elicit it? What observable evidence would support the claim? And what alternative explanations must the interpretation confront?

**Host:** Does this book establish a valid score for every individual child?

**Rowan:** No. It is a design and research account, not universal certification. Its evidence supports particular formative uses and design lessons; validity, fairness, and generalizability remain arguments that require evidence for a specific use.

**Host:** What should teachers and learners be able to contest?

**Rowan:** Consequential interpretations should be visible, explainable, and low stakes enough to correct. Teachers and learners need a route to challenge the system. Embedded assessment is not automatically fair merely because it is quiet.

**Host:** Give us the refrigerator magnet.

**Rowan:** Design the task for the claim. Treat play as evidence, not as the conclusion. Let teachers and learners inspect the inference.
