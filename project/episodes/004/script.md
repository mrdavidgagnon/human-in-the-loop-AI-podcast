# Episode 004 — The Feeling Learner and the Evidence Trail

**Host:** Welcome to Human in the Loop. Today: can a learning game notice how you feel, respond usefully, and still make a defensible claim about what you know? That sounds like one question. It is actually three questions wearing a trench coat.

**Rowan:** And the trench coat has sensors. I’m Dr. Rowan Field, a fictional composite research correspondent. I am not an author, no cited author was interviewed, and nothing I say represents author testimony. This dialogue is synthesis and paraphrase.

**Host:** One more label before we inspect the coat: this is an AI-generated summary of the cited literature. It can contain errors. It is an orientation, not a substitute for reading the sources. With that settled, Rowan, why begin with feeling?

**Rowan:** Because learning is not emotionally neutral, and games deliberately shape attention, tension, surprise, confidence, and frustration. But an emotion is neither a test score nor a tiny verdict floating above a learner’s head. It is information about an experience, interpreted in context.

**Host:** So if a player looks frustrated, we should not stamp “not learning” on the file?

**Rowan:** Correct. Productive struggle can feel frustrating. Confusion can precede insight. Delight can accompany learning, or merely excellent animation. Our first source gives us a vocabulary for avoiding that collapse. The full citation is Abir Hamrouni and Fatima Bendella, “A Taxonomy of Learner-Player’s Emotions in Serious Games,” published in the International Journal of Serious Games in 2023.

**Host:** What does their taxonomy separate?

**Rowan:** Emotions associated with learning elements from emotions associated with game elements. That distinction matters. A player might enjoy exploration but feel uncertain about the science, or dislike a time limit while understanding the content perfectly well. “Positive” and “negative” are too blunt if we do not ask: emotion about what?

**Host:** And how did the paper establish the categories?

**Rowan:** It is a conceptual synthesis of affective models and research directions. It organizes a field. It does not validate a camera-based detector, demonstrate that a system can infer a learner’s inner state, or prove that adapting to a labeled emotion improves learning.

**Host:** That boundary seems easy to lose. A neat taxonomy can look like a menu for an algorithm.

**Rowan:** Exactly. A category is not a sensor. A facial movement, pause, or repeated attempt is an observable trace. “Frustration” is an inference. “Frustration caused by the learning task” is a more specific inference. “Needs easier content” is already a decision. Each step adds assumptions.

**Host:** Our refrigerator magnet, then: traces are observed; feelings and needs are inferred.

**Rowan:** A useful magnet. Keep room beneath it for “and learners can tell you that you’re wrong.”

**Host:** If emotion is not a decorative extra, how does a designer work with it without installing a mood dashboard and declaring victory?

**Rowan:** Our second source proposes design patterns. The complete citation is Gabriel C. Natucci and Marcos A. F. Borges, “Bridging Emotional Design and Serious Games: Towards Affective Learning Design Patterns,” in the Proceedings of the Fifteenth European Conference on Game Based Learning, published in 2021.

**Host:** What is being bridged?

**Rowan:** Emotional design, cognition, pedagogy, and serious-game elements. The paper uses an integrative model of emotion in game-based learning to reason about how design choices may shape appraisals, emotions, cognitive engagement, and learning. The value is relational: do not bolt an emotion onto a mechanic. Ask how the mechanic, learning goal, feedback, and learner’s interpretation interact.

**Host:** Give me the ordinary-language version.

**Rowan:** Suppose a game says, “Wrong. Try again,” while a clock drains. That feedback, the time pressure, the learner’s confidence, and the task difficulty jointly shape the experience. Changing the color of the button is not emotional design in any meaningful sense.

**Host:** And the evidence boundary?

**Rowan:** These are conceptual design patterns, not causal guarantees. A pattern can help a team ask better questions. It cannot promise that a specific implementation will produce curiosity, reduce anxiety, or improve retention. Those outcomes need study with actual learners in an actual setting.

**Host:** Which brings us to a study that changed narrative and feedback in an augmented-reality game.

**Rowan:** Yes. Here is the full citation: Julia Flottmann, Kevin Palzer, and Daniel Bodemer, “The Impact of Narrative and Feedback on Cognitive Load and Learning in Educational AR Games,” in the Proceedings of the Nineteenth International Conference of the Learning Sciences, published by the International Society of the Learning Sciences in 2025.

**Host:** Augmented reality means digital material placed into a view of the physical environment. What did the study do?

**Rowan:** In a laboratory study with 123 participants using an educational augmented-reality game, the researchers varied whether a narrative was present and whether feedback was process-oriented. They examined cognitive load and learning. The abstract reports no significant relationship between narrative and process-oriented feedback on cognitive load and learning, while process-oriented feedback improved transfer.

**Host:** Transfer meaning learners could use what they learned beyond the exact practiced item?

**Rowan:** That is the relevant idea. The study also reports relationships among immersion, types of cognitive load, and recall. But we should resist turning one short conference report into “narrative does nothing” or “process feedback always works.” It is one laboratory sample, one implementation, particular measures, and a bounded task.

**Host:** I like that the result is not a slogan. Narrative did not simply sprinkle learning dust over the headset.

**Rowan:** Narrative is a design component, not an active ingredient with a fixed dosage. It can focus attention, provide meaning, or add extraneous material. Feedback can reveal a useful process, or become another stream of demands. Cognitive load is not a single fuel gauge; researchers distinguish load related to task complexity, avoidable demands, and productive processing, although terminology and measurement remain debated.

**Host:** So the practical question is not, “Should my game have a story?”

**Rowan:** Better questions are: what work is the story doing, what information does feedback provide, for whom, at what moment, and how will we know? The paper supplies empirical signals, not universal settings.

**Host:** This sounds like evidence-centered design. Give us the researched background, carefully, because historical verbs have sharp edges.

**Rowan:** A concise foundational introduction is Robert J. Mislevy, Russell G. Almond, and Janice F. Lukas, A Brief Introduction to Evidence-Centered Design, CSE Technical Report 632, published by the National Center for Research on Evaluation, Standards, and Student Testing at the University of California, Los Angeles, in 2004.

**Host:** What is the idea without pretending one report invented assessment?

**Rowan:** Evidence-centered design was developed through distributed work in educational assessment, strongly associated with Mislevy and colleagues. The framework asks teams to coordinate three things: the claims they want to make about learners, the evidence that would support those claims, and tasks or situations that can elicit that evidence. The 2004 report introduces that logic; it does not prove that an assessment is valid merely because its designers draw the three boxes.

**Host:** Claim, evidence, task. If the claim is “can reason with data,” clicking quickly may be a trace but not sufficient evidence.

**Rowan:** Exactly. And the task must create genuine opportunities for the relevant reasoning. Then the interpretation must confront alternatives. Did the learner understand the data, guess, exploit a mechanic, misunderstand the interface, or collaborate with a peer? Rich game telemetry can create a larger haystack without producing a better needle.

**Host:** There is also an ethical question. If a game senses behavior and perhaps emotion, what does the learner get to know or contest?

**Rowan:** A serious one. Designers should make consequential interpretations visible, minimize unnecessary data, test for uneven error, and preserve a route for teachers and learners to challenge the system. Affect inference is especially sensitive because ambiguity can be mistaken for intimacy. A system does not know you merely because it has labeled your eyebrows.

**Host:** Our final source zooms out from the product to the theory created during design.

**Rowan:** The complete citation is Andrea A. diSessa and Paul Cobb, “Ontological Innovation and the Role of Theory in Design Experiments,” published in The Journal of the Learning Sciences in 2004.

**Host:** “Ontological innovation” sounds as though the ontology has applied for a patent.

**Rowan:** It means developing explanatory constructs: proposing useful new kinds of things for a theory to talk about. DiSessa and Cobb argue that design research can do more than test whether an intervention worked. Through iterative design and analysis, researchers can develop theoretical categories that explain learning and guide consequential design.

**Host:** Connect that to our emotional evidence trail.

**Rowan:** A team might begin with broad labels such as frustration or engagement. Close study may reveal that those labels hide different phenomena: uncertainty about content, irritation with controls, strategic persistence, social embarrassment, or productive impasse. Better constructs can improve both explanation and design. But a freshly named construct is not automatically real, measurable, or generalizable.

**Host:** So design can create theory, but the theory must earn its keep.

**Rowan:** Yes. It should explain consequential patterns, inform design decisions, survive contact with data, and remain open to revision. This paper is methodological and illustrated through examples. It is not an intervention study about games, augmented reality, or emotion.

**Host:** Let me test the synthesis. Hamrouni and Bendella tell us to distinguish emotion around learning from emotion around game elements. Natucci and Borges connect emotional design with cognition and pedagogy. Flottmann, Palzer, and Bodemer provide a bounded experiment on narrative and process feedback. DiSessa and Cobb remind us that the categories themselves can and should evolve.

**Rowan:** Good. And the evidence-centered design background supplies connective tissue: decide what claim you need, what evidence could warrant it, and what task could elicit that evidence. Do not start with the data stream merely because it is available.

**Host:** Where does the human stay in the loop?

**Rowan:** At every interpretive seam. Learners describe their experience and contest misreadings. Teachers combine game evidence with context the system lacks. Designers decide which emotions and actions the game invites. Researchers test rival explanations. Institutions decide what data may be collected and what consequences an inference may carry.

**Host:** Then our final refrigerator magnet is: design the feeling; trace the evidence; limit the claim.

**Rowan:** With a smaller magnet underneath: ask the learner.
