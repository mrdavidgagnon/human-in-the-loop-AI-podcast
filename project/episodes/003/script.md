**Host:** Good. Then what makes a game educational?

**Rowan:** Not points, not a quiz wearing a cape, and not the presence of a chatbot. A game becomes part of education when its activity is aligned with a learning goal, when learners have useful support, and when someone can examine evidence of what they understood or can do. Sometimes the mechanic embodies the idea. Sometimes the game creates an experience that needs explanation, reflection, and instruction around it.

**Host:** So a game can be enjoyable, informative, and educationally unhelpful all at once?

**Rowan:** Quite easily. Enjoyment is worth knowing about, but it is not a learning outcome. The better question is: what did the design invite the learner to notice, practise, explain, revise, or transfer—and what evidence supports that inference?

**Host:** Let us meet the first source, with the full citation in the room.

**Rowan:** Kun Huang and Ching-Huei Chen wrote “Instructional Video and GenAI-Supported Chatbot in Digital Game-Based Learning: Influences on Science Learning, Cognitive Load and Game Behaviours,” published in the *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* in 2025. They studied 160 seventh-grade students using a two-by-two design: instructional videos present or absent, and a generative-AI-supported chatbot present or absent.

**Host:** Two species of help: one prepared and systematic, one conversational and adaptive. What happened?

**Rowan:** In this science-game setting, the instructional-video groups performed better on science learning than groups without the videos. The combination of videos and chatbot produced the lowest reported extraneous cognitive load, while the chatbot-only group displayed more fragmented inquiry patterns in the game traces.

**Host:** That is wonderfully inconvenient for the story in which conversational AI simply makes every learning environment better.

**Rowan:** Useful evidence often is. The claim is not that chatbots are bad. It is that adaptive language is not the same as coherent instructional support. A response can sound helpful locally while scattering the learner's attention across the larger inquiry. Prepared scaffolding can make underlying science concepts and the structure of the task more visible.

**Host:** And the boundary?

**Rowan:** One study, one age group, one science-game context, and one particular arrangement of tools. It does not establish a general effect for GenAI chatbots, other subjects, or other learners. It certainly does not show that a chatbot replaces teachers or instructional design. Trace patterns also remain interpretations: fragmented action is evidence about behaviour in the system, not direct observation of a learner's mind.

**Host:** What question should a teacher ask when the chatbot gives a plausible answer?

**Rowan:** “Plausible for what next action?” Does it direct attention to the scientific relationship? Does it preserve productive struggle? Can the learner tell why the suggestion matters? And can the teacher see and contest what the system is doing? Fluency is a property of the response. Educational usefulness is a relationship among response, learner, goal, and context.

**Host:** I hear a broader idea hiding there: scaffolding. Has the word become so common that we forget it is a claim about temporary support?

**Rowan:** Exactly. For authoritative background, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine published *How People Learn II: Learners, Contexts, and Cultures* through the National Academies Press in Washington, D.C., in 2018. Its technology chapter says learning technologies should be aligned with learning goals and contexts and can create opportunities for feedback, reflection, and revision. It also stresses implementation support for learners and educators.

**Host:** So the background source does not prove anything about Huang and Chen's game.

**Rowan:** Correct. It gives us a disciplined orientation, not an extra experimental result. The historical development of “scaffolding” is distributed across educational and psychological traditions, so we are not giving it a tidy single-inventor story. The practical meaning here is bounded support that helps a learner perform and understand activity they could not yet manage alone, with the aim of increasing independence rather than permanent dependence.

**Host:** A chatbot that never fades is less scaffolding than architectural load-bearing wall.

**Rowan:** And a wall whose building code is still in beta.

**Host:** The second focal source moves the work outside the game. Full citation, please.

**Rowan:** Yoon Jeon Kim and Oleg Pavlov wrote “Game-based structural debriefing: How can teachers design game-based curricula for systems thinking?”, published in *Information and Learning Sciences* in 2019. They developed game-based structural debriefing, or G.B.S.D., to connect gameplay with systems-thinking goals using system-dynamics visualisations and debriefing activities.

**Host:** What does structural debriefing add that ordinary “How did everyone enjoy the game?” discussion does not?

**Rowan:** It asks learners to reconstruct causal structure. What variables changed? Which relationships drove the outcome? Where were the feedback loops, accumulations, and delays? The game produces an experience; the debrief helps the class turn that experience into an inspectable model. The teacher coordinates between the simulated system, disciplinary language, and learners' explanations.

**Host:** How was the framework developed?

**Rowan:** Through design-based research over about twelve months: one focus-group interview, three design workshops, iterative curriculum development, and implementation by three participating teachers in a middle-school life-science ecosystem unit. The teachers also made additional materials for the needs and limits of their classrooms.

**Host:** That adaptation seems important. The framework did not stroll into three rooms and teach identically while the teachers stood respectfully behind a fern.

**Rowan:** Precisely. The evidence supports the framework's utility and flexibility as a design resource. It does not provide a universal effect size for debriefing or prove that any systems game will produce systems thinking. A three-teacher implementation can demonstrate possibility and expose design requirements; it cannot represent every classroom.

**Host:** And it makes teacher judgment visible rather than treating variation as contamination.

**Rowan:** Yes. Teachers notice vocabulary, time pressure, social dynamics, prior knowledge, and local curriculum constraints. Their additions are not merely imperfections around a pristine technology. They are part of how an educational activity becomes usable. That is the claim-plus-boundary pattern: implementation evidence can tell us how something was made to work, while remaining modest about general effects.

**Host:** The phrase “systems thinking” also invites extravagant claims. What is safe to say?

**Rowan:** Here it means examining interdependent elements and dynamic causal relationships rather than treating events as isolated. G.B.S.D. borrows visual tools from system dynamics to make that structure discussable. We should not claim that one diagram captures an ecosystem, or that drawing arrows guarantees transfer. A model is useful because it can be explained, tested, and revised—not because it resembles complexity from a tasteful distance.

**Host:** Put the sources together. What does the interview reveal?

**Rowan:** Huang and Chen show that scaffold type matters and that conversational adaptivity does not automatically produce coherent inquiry. Kim and Pavlov show teachers co-designing a structured bridge between gameplay and systems explanation. The National Academies background places these article-focused works within alignment, context, support, feedback, reflection, and revision.

**Host:** So when does a game need a teacher?

**Rowan:** Not only when the software fails. A game needs a teacher when goals must be interpreted, supports coordinated, experiences connected to disciplinary ideas, evidence questioned, adaptations contested, and learners recognised as more than traces. Technology can redistribute that work. It does not make the work disappear.

**Host:** Refrigerator magnet?

**Rowan:** Let the game create the experience. Let the scaffold support the next move. Let the debrief turn action into explanation. Let a human remain responsible for the inference.
