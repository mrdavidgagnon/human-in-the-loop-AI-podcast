     - Evidence: Supports the **descriptive finding** that on four deliberately
     subjective codes (Emotional Responses, Use of Language, Playful References,
     Cultural References) applied to 200 lines of U.S. middle schoolers' typed
     in-game observations, cross-cultural agreement among three pairs of
     experienced coders (China, India, United States) was low — all twelve pairwise
     Cohen's kappa values fell between 0.09 and 0.44, below the κ ≥ 0.7
     convention the paper itself invokes. Supports the finding that **GPT-4o
     agreed more closely with the U.S. coder pair than with either non-Western
     pair on three of four constructs** under baseline prompting (U.S. κ = 0.60,
     0.56, 0.31, 0.54), with Playful References the exception (IND 0.44 > U.S.
     0.31); and that this pattern **persisted under culture-specific prompting**,
     where GPT-4o reached κ ≥ 0.7 with no human pair on any construct, and
     agreement with the U.S. pair *fell* for Emotional Responses (0.60 → 0.55)
     and Cultural References (0.54 → 0.41). Supports a **thematic
     characterisation** of four mechanisms behind cross-cultural disagreement —
     unfamiliar cultural phenomenon, cultural habituation, cultural projection,
     and cultural framing — constructed (the authors' word) from artifacts of a
     60-minute collective coder discussion.
     - Boundaries: **Does not establish** that national culture determines coding
     decisions — the paper's own gamer-culture example shows two U.S. coders
     diverging on subcultural grounds. **Does not establish** that cultural
     prompting cannot work: cultural framing and prompt language (Chinese,
     English, Hindi) were manipulated simultaneously, so the design cannot
     isolate cultural framing from language-dependent model behaviour, and only
     one model (GPT-4o, retained for consistency as the most recent model
     available when analysis began) at one point in time was tested. **Does not
     establish accuracy**: the reference standard was each pair's own
     socially-moderated consensus, which the authors explicitly decline to treat
     as objective or universally correct, so GPT-4o's closer agreement with the
     U.S. pair is alignment, not correctness. **No learning outcome of any kind
     was measured** — this is a study of researchers, using students' words as
     material. Sample limits are narrow and author-acknowledged: students drawn
     primarily from one cultural (U.S.) and linguistic (English) background; only
     three culturally distinct coder pairs (Europe, Latin America, Africa and
     indigenous voices absent); **only two coders per cultural group**, so results
     reflect the degree of alignment with these participating coders rather than
     with broader national or cultural perspectives; and the codebook was
     developed primarily by etic coders, with emic oversight invited. The
     qualitative account of *why* the model shifted under prompting rests in part
     on GPT-4o's own generated explanations, which the authors treat as
     potentially informative artifacts rather than definitive evidence of the
     processes underlying its outputs. Reported rationale rankings are **perceived**
     importance and frequency on a 10-point scale, not measured counts.
